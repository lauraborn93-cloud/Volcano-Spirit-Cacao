What Is Artificial Chocolate Made Of?

You probably don't want to know, but we'll tell you anyway.

← Back to Blog · By Laura Born · June 16, 2026

Artificial chocolate, also called imitation chocolate or compound chocolate, is a chocolate-flavored product that replaces real cocoa butter with cheaper vegetable fats like palm or coconut oil, often alongside artificial flavoring designed to mimic the taste of cacao. It's legal to sell, common in candy and baked goods, and in many cases doesn't contain much real cacao at all.

Wait, chocolate can be "fake"?

Here's a strange truth most people never think to question: the chocolate aisle is full of products that aren't, technically, chocolate.

Pick up almost any candy bar, and there's a decent chance what you're holding is what the industry calls compound chocolate, a cheaper imitation built to taste like chocolate without containing much of what makes chocolate, chocolate. It melts the same. It looks the same on the shelf. But underneath the wrapper, the ingredient list tells a very different story than the one cacao has been telling for the last 4,000 years.

We think that's worth talking about plainly. Not to scare anyone off their favorite candy bar, but because once you understand what "artificial chocolate" actually is, you start to see real cacao in a completely different light: the kind grown the way it always was, by the families who've never stopped. Learn about real cacao here.

What is artificial chocolate made of?

Artificial chocolate is built to imitate real chocolate's taste and texture, while swapping out its most expensive and most important ingredient: cocoa butter.

In genuine chocolate, that rich melt-in-your-mouth quality comes from cocoa butter, the natural fat pressed from cacao beans. It's expensive, temperature-sensitive, and finicky to work with. So manufacturers looking to cut costs replace it with cheaper, more stable vegetable fats: palm oil, coconut oil, or partially hydrogenated soybean oil are common choices.

Once the cocoa butter is gone, the product no longer tastes much like cacao on its own, so artificial flavoring is added back in to recreate that familiar "chocolate" taste. The result is something the industry calls compound chocolate: a mixture of sugar, vegetable fat, a small amount of cocoa powder (for color and a faint chocolate note), and flavoring, engineered to behave like chocolate without the cost or complexity of the real thing.

Compound chocolate is permitted and widely used, especially in baking, candy coatings, and mass-market confectionery, because it's cheaper, doesn't require the same temperature control as real chocolate, and has a longer shelf life. But it is, by definition, an imitation.

Is there such a thing as "imitation chocolate"?

Yes, and it's more common than most people realize. "Imitation chocolate" and "compound chocolate" describe largely the same thing: a chocolate-flavored coating or candy made without real cocoa butter.

You'll find it most often in:

  • Chocolate-flavored candy coatings (think the shell on some inexpensive chocolate-covered treats)
  • Budget baking chips marketed as "chocolate-flavored" rather than simply "chocolate"
  • Some mass-produced candy bars, where compound chocolate keeps costs down at scale
  • Vending machine and bulk candy, where shelf-stability matters more than authenticity

The giveaway is almost always on the ingredient label. If "vegetable oil," "palm kernel oil," or "cocoa powder" appears prominently while cocoa butter is absent or listed far down the list, you're very likely looking at compound chocolate rather than real chocolate.

The brand everyone talks about: is Hershey's "imitation chocolate"?

This is one of the most common questions people ask once they start digging into this topic, and the honest answer is: it depends on the product, not the brand as a whole.

Hershey's classic milk chocolate bar does contain real cocoa butter and is legally classified as chocolate in the United States, under FDA standards of identity. It's not compound chocolate. What it is, though, is a long way from cacao in its original form: heavily processed, sweetened, and built for mass production rather than flavor complexity or ceremony.

Some of Hershey's other products, particularly certain coatings and seasonal items, do use compound chocolate or chocolate-flavored coatings rather than real chocolate, so the honest answer isn't a simple yes or no. It's "check the ingredient label for that specific product," which, frankly, is good advice for any chocolate brand on the shelf.

What's the difference between real chocolate and the cacao it came from?

This is the part that doesn't get talked about enough, because even "real" chocolate, legally speaking, is already several steps removed from the plant it started as.

Cacao, in its original form, is whole: fermented, dried, and ground from the bean, with nothing added or removed. By the time it becomes a chocolate bar, even a good one, sugar has been added, the proportions have been engineered for shelf life and mouthfeel, and in many cases, the fat content has been adjusted with additional cocoa butter or other oils. "Real" chocolate isn't artificial. But it's also not cacao anymore. It's a confection made from cacao.

Compound chocolate takes that distance one step further, replacing the one ingredient (cocoa butter) that still connected the candy bar to the actual cacao plant.

What is the unhealthiest type of chocolate to eat?

We're not in a position to make medical claims about "healthy" or "unhealthy" foods. That's not something a cacao brand should be deciding for you. What we can point to is composition.

Generally speaking, products furthest from real cacao, heavily sweetened compound chocolate and imitation coatings, tend to contain the least cocoa, the most added sugar and saturated fat, and the fewest of the naturally occurring compounds (like flavanols) that whole cacao contains. Products closer to whole, minimally processed cacao retain more of what the bean naturally provides, simply because less has been removed or replaced. Beyond that, what's "best" depends on what you're looking for: a treat, a baking ingredient, or a whole-food cacao experience. That's a personal call, not one we'll make for you.

How to tell if your chocolate is real

A few quick things worth checking next time you're holding a bar:

  • Check for cocoa butter. If it's not listed, or listed after vegetable oil, palm oil, or "vegetable fat," you're likely looking at compound chocolate.
  • Check the cocoa percentage. Genuine dark chocolate lists a cocoa solids percentage prominently. Compound chocolate rarely does, because there often isn't much to boast about.
  • Check how it melts. Real chocolate, made with cocoa butter, melts smoothly at body temperature. Compound chocolate, made with more heat-stable fats, often feels slightly waxy or doesn't melt the same way on your tongue.
  • Check where it came from. This is the one most guides skip. A chocolate bar can be "real" by legal definition and still come from cacao grown in a cleared monoculture, harvested by underpaid labor, and processed industrially to strip out everything except what's needed to hit a flavor profile. Legally real doesn't always mean honestly sourced.

So is "artificial chocolate" actually a conspiracy?

No, and we want to be straightforward here, because this question gets asked more than you'd think. Compound and imitation chocolate aren't secret or illegal. They're a known, regulated category of food product, used deliberately by manufacturers because they're cheaper and more stable than real chocolate. It's disclosed on the ingredient label, even if most people never read it closely enough to notice.

What feels like a "conspiracy" is really just a lack of attention. Chocolate marketing leans hard on imagery of melting bars and rich cacao fields, while the actual ingredient list quietly tells a much plainer story. The fix isn't outrage. It's just looking a little closer at what you're buying.

What to do about artificial chocolate

Once you start checking labels, you start noticing how far most "chocolate" has drifted from the plant it's named after. Read the ingredients, ask where things came from, and when you want the real thing, go looking for cacao that hasn't had anything taken away from it.
